## Why Psychology Is Time-Dependent

The same trader behaves differently at 9:00 AM, 1:05 PM and 7:40 PM. Quiet Asia invites boredom; London's opening spike invites fear of missing out; a sharp New York reversal after data invites panic or revenge. Knowing **when** each temptation appears lets you set a rule **before** it does.

<Callout type="warning" title="Toned down">
  The original described who is "trapped" at each session ("retail" vs "smart money"). Nobody can see other traders'
  positions or feelings from a chart. This lesson sticks to what you can observe: **your own decisions** and what price
  did next.
</Callout>

## The Mood of Each Session

| Session / time (BD, summer) | What price is often like              | The temptation                    | The rule that counters it                          |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Asia, 6:00 AM – 12:30 PM    | Small ranges, slow candles            | Boredom — trading anything        | Mark levels and write the bias; no trades          |
| London open, 1:00 – 1:30 PM | Fast first moves, sometimes reversed  | FOMO — chasing the first spike    | Watch for 30 minutes; trade closes, not touches    |
| After a sweep               | Sharp reversal against the first move | Panic — exiting or flipping late  | Stop and size set before entry; no mid-trade edits |
| New York, 6:30 PM data      | Sudden spikes, spreads widen          | Chasing the spike; revenge trades | No entry for the first minutes; max trades per day |
| After 10:00 PM              | Quieter; London has closed            | "One more trade" to fix the day   | Fixed stop time                                    |

C could still lose; the point is that C's loss would be a _planned_ loss, and C would have taken no trade at all on days when the conditions never appeared.

## A Pre-Session Rule Sheet

Write it once and read it at the start of each session:

1. **Bias:** long / short / none (and the level that cancels it).
2. **Window:** the hours I trade; the hour I stop.
3. **Trigger:** "a close back inside and a CHOCH" (or your own rule).
4. **Risk:** fixed per trade, maximum trades, maximum daily loss.
5. **After a stop-out:** wait 15 minutes; the next trade must meet all of the above.

### Example (no chart) — New York data, a revenge trade avoided

At 6:30 PM BD US data spikes EUR/USD 30 pips and the first trade of the evening (planned, 1R) is stopped. The old habit: re-enter immediately, double size. The rule sheet says: wait 15 minutes, require a close back inside and a CHOCH, same size. At 7:05 PM price closes back inside and forms a CHOCH; the planned entry is **1.0852**, stop **1.0836**, target **1.0884** (risk 16, reward 32, **1 : 2.0**). Whether or not it works, the rule held.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake                              | Fix                                             |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Chasing the first London spike       | Wait 30 minutes; trade the close                |
| Trading Asia out of boredom          | Mark levels; don't trade                        |
| Doubling size after a loss           | Fixed size; a stop-out triggers a wait          |
| Moving the stop mid-trade            | Set it before entry; only move it toward profit |
| "One more trade" after the stop time | Alarm at the stop time; close the platform      |

## Practice

1. For 15 trades, record the time, the emotion at entry (calm, FOMO, boredom, revenge) and whether the trade met your written rule.
2. Compare the average result of rule-based trades against emotional ones.
3. Write the one rule that would have prevented your most common emotional entry.
4. Log it in your [trading journal](/en/tools/journal-template).
