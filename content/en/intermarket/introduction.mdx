## What Is Intermarket Analysis?

**Intermarket analysis** looks at how different markets — currencies, bonds, stocks, commodities — move **in relation to each other**, and uses that as **context** for the market you trade. A currency pair doesn't move in isolation: US interest rates, the dollar, risk appetite and commodity prices all feed into it.

<Callout type="warning" title="Corrected and toned down">
  The original said "no institutional fund trades a single instrument in isolation" and that correlations are what
  "separate professionals from retail". We don't have a source for either. Many traders, professional and otherwise,
  trade one market well. Intermarket analysis is a way to add context, not a requirement, and not an edge by itself.
</Callout>

## Why Markets Are Linked

| Link               | Common tendency (not a rule)                                                     |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The US dollar**  | It is on one side of most major pairs, so dollar moves show up across them       |
| **Interest rates** | Higher expected US rates often support the dollar; lower often weigh on it       |
| **Risk appetite**  | In risk-off periods stocks often fall while the dollar, yen and franc often firm |
| **Commodities**    | Oil can matter for CAD and NOK; gold is often negatively related to the dollar   |

Each of these has periods when it doesn't hold. They are starting hypotheses to check on your own data.

## Measuring a Relationship

**Correlation** is a number from **−1** (move in opposite directions) to **+1** (move together); **0** means no linear relationship. Use the correlation of **returns** (daily percent changes), not of price levels, which can look related just because both trend.

| Value        | Reading                       |
| ------------ | ----------------------------- |
| −1 to −0.7   | Strong inverse                |
| −0.7 to −0.3 | Moderate inverse              |
| −0.3 to +0.3 | Weak or none                  |
| +0.3 to +1   | Positive, increasingly strong |

Two lessons: a past correlation is not a promise, and a **rolling** correlation shows when the relationship is changing, but only after the fact.

## A Workable Routine

| Step | What to do                                                                          |
| ---- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | Pick one or two related markets for the pair you trade (see the next lessons)       |
| 2    | Measure correlation of returns on the window you trade (for example 20 and 60 days) |
| 3    | Note whether the relationship is stable or changing                                 |
| 4    | Use it as **context**: does the related market support or contradict your idea?     |
| 5    | If they disagree, size down or wait — don't treat either as the answer              |

### Example (no chart) — context, not a signal

You plan to buy EUR/USD at **1.0870** after a pullback; stop **1.0850**, target **1.0910**: risk 20 pips, reward 40, **1 : 2.0** planned. The dollar index is rising and its 20-day correlation with EUR/USD is −0.8. The related market contradicts your idea, so you halve the size or wait for a clearer setup. If its correlation had been near zero, you would give the dollar index less weight.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake                           | Fix                                                  |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Treating correlation as causation | A shared driver or coincidence can produce it        |
| Using price-level correlation     | Use returns                                          |
| Assuming a relationship is fixed  | Re-measure; watch the rolling value                  |
| Using one correlation window      | Compare a short and a long window                    |
| Trading only on the other market  | Your own market's structure and risk plan come first |

## Practice

1. Download daily closes for EUR/USD and the dollar index for six months. Compute the correlation of daily returns.
2. Compute it for the last 20 days and the last 60 days. Are they the same?
3. Pick one day where the two disagreed. What did EUR/USD do next?
4. Log it in your [trading journal](/en/tools/journal-template).

## Summary

| Idea        | Detail                                                 |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Intermarket | Using related markets as context for the one you trade |
| Correlation | −1 to +1, measured on returns, not price levels        |
| Changes     | Relationships strengthen, weaken and break; re-measure |
| Use         | One input among several, never a rule                  |
