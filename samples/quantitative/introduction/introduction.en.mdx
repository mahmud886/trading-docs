## What Is Quantitative Trading?

**Quantitative (systematic) trading** means trading from **explicit rules** that can be tested: exactly when to enter, where the stop and target go, and how much to risk. Because the rules are written down, they can be measured on past data, and results can be judged over many trades instead of by the last few.

<Callout type="warning" title="Corrected and toned down">
  The original said "if you cannot express your edge as a number, you don't have an edge" and gave fixed rules such as
  "minimum 100+ trades" and "reduce size beyond 1 SD". A rule-based approach is useful, but a number does not
  guarantee an edge, and the sample you need depends on how big the edge is and how noisy the results are. Treat the
  thresholds in the next lessons as starting points to test, not standards.
</Callout>

## Why a Small Edge Needs a Large Sample

Take a strategy that wins **45%** of the time for **+1.5R** and loses **55%** of the time for **−1R**. Its expectancy per trade is

> 0.45 × 1.5 − 0.55 × 1 = **+0.125R**

That is a real, positive edge, but small compared with the noise in each trade.

<Callout type="info" title="About this chart">
  The paths are a **simulation** of independent trades with fixed probabilities. The bars are **exact** probabilities
  from the binomial distribution (a 5,000-path simulation agrees within a few points). Real trading adds costs and
  changing conditions.

| Trades | Probability the cumulative result is positive |
| ------ | --------------------------------------------- |
| 10     | 50%                                           |
| 20     | 59%                                           |
| 50     | 71%                                           |
| 100    | 82%                                           |
| 200    | 91%                                           |
| 500    | 99%                                           |

After 10 trades a strategy with a real edge is **as likely to be behind as ahead**; even after 100 trades it is behind about 18% of the time. In the 30 simulated paths, 6 spend time more than 5R below zero before recovering. This is why judging a system on a few trades is unreliable, in both directions: a good system can look bad, and a worthless one can look good.

## A Systematic Workflow

| Step | What to do                                                                  |
| ---- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | Write the idea as rules: entry, stop, target, size                          |
| 2    | Measure expectancy on historical data, including costs                      |
| 3    | Test on data the rules were not built on                                    |
| 4    | Estimate how bad a bad run could be (Monte Carlo)                           |
| 5    | Trade small, record every trade, and compare with the expected behaviour    |

### Example (no chart) — one rule set

Rules: buy a retest of a broken level, stop **20 pips** below, target **30 pips** above: **1 : 1.5** planned. Entry **1.0850**, stop **1.0830**, target **1.0880**. With a 45% win rate the expectancy is +0.125R; with a 38% win rate it would be 0.38 × 1.5 − 0.62 × 1 = −0.05R. The planned ratio alone does not decide: the win rate does too, and it needs a sample to estimate.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake                                         | Fix                                                        |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Judging a system on 10–20 trades                | Use a larger sample and report the uncertainty             |
| Changing the rules after each loss              | Change them only in a planned review                       |
| Ignoring costs                                  | Include spread, commission and slippage                    |
| Assuming a positive number means an edge        | Test on data it wasn't fitted to                           |
| Risking too much per trade                      | See [Kelly Criterion](/en/quantitative/kelly-criterion)    |

## Practice

1. Write one strategy as explicit rules in a sentence or two. Could someone else apply them and reach the same trades?
2. Compute the expectancy from your last 30 trades. How uncertain is it?
3. Using the 45% / +1.5R example, compute the expectancy at win rates of 35%, 40% and 45%. Where does it cross zero?
4. Log it in your [trading journal](/en/tools/journal-template).
