## The Idea

A series is **mean-reverting** if, after moving away from its average, it tends to come back. A mean-reversion strategy **fades** stretched moves: sell when price is far above its average, buy when far below, and exit near the average.

<Callout type="warning" title="Corrected and toned down">
  The original described mean reversion as "strategies used by quantitative funds" including statistical arbitrage. These
  strategies do exist, but whether a *particular* series reverts is an empirical question, and many do not.
  Single-instrument mean reversion in a trending market is one of the classic ways to lose money. This lesson
  shows both outcomes on synthetic data.
</Callout>

## The Z-Score

> **z = (price − rolling mean) ÷ rolling standard deviation**

A z of +2 means price is two standard deviations above its recent average. In a bell-shaped distribution about 5% of observations lie beyond ±2, but market data is not that tidy: stretched readings cluster, and a z of 3 or 4 does happen.

| Parameter     | Typical test range                                    | Trade-off                                                     |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Window        | 20 to 60 bars                                         | Short = noisy; long = slow to adapt                           |
| Entry z       | ±1.5 to ±2.5                                          | Higher = fewer, more stretched trades                         |
| Exit z        | 0 (the mean) or ±0.5                                  | Exiting early reduces profit per trade                        |
| Stop z        | ±3 to ±4, or a fixed distance                         | The stop is what limits the cost of being wrong               |

## The Same Rule on Two Series

Rule: window 40; short at z > +2, long at z < −2; exit when z crosses 0; stop if z reaches 3.5 against the position (checked on closes); no costs.

| --------------------- | ------ | ------- | --------------- | ----------- |
| Mean-reverting        | 11     | 11      | +34             | +2 (all won) |
| Random walk           | 7      | 5       | −20             | −27         |

On the random walk **most trades still win** (5 of 7), but the two losers are far bigger than the winners, and one of them is a stop-out of 27 points that erases the rest. A high win rate does not make the strategy sound: **the shape of the losses matters as much as the win rate** (see [Expectancy](/en/quantitative/expectancy-formula)).

## Is the Series Mean-Reverting?

A simple model: **x(t+1) − x(t) = −θ · (x(t) − mean) + noise**. θ is the pull back to the mean per bar. For the series above θ = 0.12, which gives a **half-life** of ln(2) ÷ θ ≈ **5.4 bars**: half of a deviation is gone after about five bars.

| Check                           | What to look for                                                          |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Half-life                       | A few bars to a few weeks, shorter than your holding period tolerates     |
| Statistical test                | An augmented Dickey–Fuller test on the series or spread rejects a random walk |
| Stability                       | The estimate holds across different periods, not just one                 |
| Regime                          | A trending market can break a relationship that held for years            |

## Pairs Trading

**Pairs trading** applies the idea to the **spread** between two related instruments (for example two similar stocks, or a stock and its sector index). The spread can be mean-reverting even when each leg trends. You trade the spread's z-score: long one leg and short the other when the spread is stretched. The relationship must be tested (the legs need to be **cointegrated**) and monitored, because it can break.

### Example (no chart) — fading a stretch on a single instrument

EUR/USD trades at **1.0900** while its 40-bar mean is **1.0860** and the standard deviation is 0.0018: z = (1.0900 − 1.0860) ÷ 0.0018 = **+2.2**. Plan: sell **1.0900**, target **1.0860** (the mean, 40 pips), stop at z = 3.5, i.e. 1.0860 + 3.5 × 0.0018 = **1.0923** (23 pips): **1 : 1.7** planned. If a news event is driving the move rather than noise, the stop is the plan's protection.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake                                         | Fix                                                        |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fading a trend because z is high                | Test that the series actually reverts first                |
| No stop                                         | The one trade that doesn't revert can cost more than all the winners |
| Judging by win rate                             | Look at expectancy and the worst loss                      |
| Using a mean that includes the move itself      | Test different windows and keep one                        |
| Pairs without a cointegration check             | Test the spread, and re-test regularly                     |

## Practice

1. Take daily closes of a pair for two years. Compute a 40-day z-score. How often does |z| exceed 2?
2. After |z| > 2, what was the average change over the next 5 days in the direction of the mean?
3. Fit θ with a regression of daily changes on the previous deviation, and compute the half-life. Is it stable across halves of the sample?
4. Log it in your [trading journal](/en/tools/journal-template).
