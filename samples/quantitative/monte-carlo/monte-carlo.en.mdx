## Why Monte Carlo?

A backtest is **one path**: one particular order in which the trades happened. The same trades in a different order, or a different sample from the same edge, would give a different equity curve and a different drawdown. **Monte Carlo** simulation generates many such paths so you can see the **range** of outcomes, not just the one that happened.

<Callout type="warning" title="Corrected and toned down">
  The original said Monte Carlo shows "ruin risk" and "confidence intervals" for real trading. Resampling only
  re-orders or re-draws **the trades you already have**. It cannot produce a loss worse than the worst trade in your
  list, and it assumes the future will look like the sample. Treat the output as a rough guide to path risk, not a
  measure of true ruin probability.
</Callout>

---

## The Bootstrap Procedure

| Step | What to do                                                                                    |
| ---- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | Collect the R-multiple of every trade (result ÷ amount risked)                                |
| 2    | Draw trades **with replacement** to build a new sequence of the same length                    |
| 3    | Compute that path's final result and **maximum drawdown** (largest peak-to-trough fall, in R)  |
| 4    | Repeat thousands of times                                                                     |
| 5    | Read percentiles: 5th, 50th (median) and 95th                                                  |

Variations: **shuffle without replacement** (keeps the same set of trades and only tests the order), or **block bootstrap** (resample runs of trades to preserve clustering).

| ----------------------------------------- | -------------- | ------ | --------------- |
| Final result after 100 trades             | −8R            | +12R   | +32R            |
| Maximum drawdown                          | 5R             | 9R     | 18R             |

Other results: **12%** of resampled paths end **below zero** even though the expectancy is positive, and **12%** have a drawdown **above 15R**. The 95th-percentile drawdown (18R) is **more than twice** the 7.5R the backtest happened to show: the backtest was one fairly kind path.

## From R to Account Drawdown

A drawdown in R becomes a percentage of the account once you choose the **risk per trade**: roughly 1 − (1 − r)^D, where *r* is the fraction risked and *D* the drawdown in R (it compounds).

| Risk per trade | 9R (median) | 18R (95th percentile) |
| -------------- | ----------- | --------------------- |
| 0.5%           | 4.4%        | 8.6%                  |
| 1%             | 8.6%        | 16.5%                 |
| 2%             | 16.6%       | 30.5%                 |
| 3%             | 24.0%       | 42.2%                 |

### Example (no chart) — choosing the risk per trade

You can tolerate a drawdown of **20%** at the 95th percentile. Solve 1 − (1 − r)^18 = 0.20: r = 1 − 0.8^(1/18) ≈ **1.23%**, so risk about **1.2%** per trade. A trade: entry **1.0850**, stop **1.0830**, target **1.0880** (**1 : 1.5** planned); on a 10,000 account that risks 120 and sizes the position at 120 ÷ 20 pips = 6 per pip. If the live drawdown passes the simulated 95th percentile, treat it as a sign the edge or the estimate may have changed.

## Limits

| Limit                                         | Detail                                                                |
| --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Only the trades you have                      | No loss bigger than the worst in your sample                          |
| Independence                                  | Plain resampling ignores clustering of losses; use a block bootstrap  |
| Small samples                                 | 100 trades gives wide, noisy percentiles; more trades narrow them     |
| The edge may change                           | Resampling assumes the future matches the sample                      |
| Costs and slippage                            | Use trades after costs                                                |

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake                                         | Fix                                                        |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Reporting only the backtest drawdown            | Report the percentile range from Monte Carlo               |
| Ignoring that drawdown compounds                | Convert R to % with the risk per trade                     |
| Using trades before costs                       | Use after-cost R                                           |
| Treating 95th percentile as the worst case      | The tail can be worse; leave margin                        |
| Resampling a rule you optimised on the same data | Resample out-of-sample trades                              |

## Practice

1. Export your trades as R-multiples. Resample 5,000 paths of 100 trades and record the 5th, 50th and 95th percentile of final R and max drawdown.
2. Convert the 95th-percentile drawdown to a percentage at your usual risk per trade.
3. Compare with the worst drawdown you have actually seen. Is it inside the range?
4. Log it in your [trading journal](/en/tools/journal-template).
