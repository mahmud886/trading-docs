## What Is the Wyckoff Method?

Richard Wyckoff (1873 – 1934) was a US stock trader and publisher who wrote courses on reading price and volume. His method is a way of asking one question: **who is in control — buyers or sellers — and is that about to change?** He answered it with price, volume and time.

Later schools — including the ideas behind SMC and ICT — borrow heavily from his vocabulary (accumulation, distribution, spring, upthrust), though they are not the same thing.

<Callout type="warning" title="Toned down">
  The original said Wyckoff developed the method "by studying how JP Morgan and Jesse Livermore manipulated markets"
  and that modern SMC/ICT are "DIRECTLY derived" from it. Wyckoff did write about observing large operators, and the
  vocabulary overlaps — but the historical claims about specific people and the direct-lineage claim are not
  something we can source. Treat the method as a framework for reading supply and demand.
</Callout>

## The Three Laws

| Law                          | Statement                                                                 | What you look at              |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| **Supply and demand**        | Price rises when demand exceeds supply, falls when supply exceeds demand; balance gives a range | Trends vs ranges      |
| **Cause and effect**         | The size of a move is related to the preparation (the range) that came before it | Width/duration of a range |
| **Effort versus result**     | Volume (effort) should match price progress (result); a mismatch is a clue | Volume vs price change        |

<Callout type="info" title="About this chart">
  The data is **synthetic and idealised**: one tidy cycle built to show the pattern. Real cycles are messier, and
  volume doesn't always behave this neatly.
</Callout>

| Phase             | What price does                   | What the evidence says (idealised)                       |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Markdown**      | Falls in a trend                  | Supply in control; volume rises into the low             |
| **Accumulation**  | Moves sideways near the low (36 – 42 here) | Heavy volume, little progress: supply absorbed |
| **Markup**        | Rises in a trend                  | Demand in control; volume on rallies                     |
| **Distribution**  | Moves sideways near the high (55 – 59 here) | Heavy volume, little progress: demand met by supply |

The key idea: **trends are separated by ranges, and the ranges are where the next trend is prepared.** The chart's two ranges are about the same width (~5 – 6 points) and carry the heaviest volume (on this synthetic data, around 1,900 versus 1,100 – 1,400 in the markup).

## Using It Without Forcing It

1. **Identify the phase first:** trend, or range? If a range, near a low or a high?
2. **Then the events:** within a range, which of the textbook events (selling climax, spring, upthrust …) have appeared? See [Accumulation Schematic](/en/wyckoff/accumulation-schematic) and [Distribution Schematic](/en/wyckoff/distribution-schematic).
3. **Then the evidence:** does volume agree with price? See [Effort vs Result](/en/wyckoff/effort-vs-result).
4. **Then the plan:** an entry, a stop beyond the invalidating event, and a target from the range's width — see [Cause and Effect](/en/wyckoff/cause-effect).

### Example (no chart) — a long from an accumulation range

A stock trades between **36.2 and 41.8** for 22 days after a long decline. A spring dips to **35.8** on lower volume and closes back at **38.5**, then price breaks above 41.8 on rising volume and pulls back to **41.0** on light volume. Buy **41.2**, stop **39.6** (below the pullback low), target **47.0** (range width ≈ 5.6, projected one-and-a-bit times up from the breakout): risk 1.6, reward 5.8, **1 : 3.6** planned — if the evidence appears; if not, no trade.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake                                           | Fix                                                    |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Forcing every range into a textbook schematic     | Identify the phase; treat events as clues              |
| Naming events (SC, ST, LPS) before the phase      | Phase first, events second                             |
| Calling any sideways move "accumulation"          | It needs a prior decline and evidence of absorption    |
| Ignoring volume                                   | Wyckoff is price **and** volume                        |
| Treating the schematic as a forecast              | It's a hypothesis with a level that proves it wrong    |

## Practice

1. On 10 daily charts, mark the trends and ranges. Name the phase of each range (accumulation or distribution).
2. For each range, note the width in price and the number of bars.
3. Note the volume inside the range versus the trend before and after.
4. Log it in your [trading journal](/en/tools/journal-template).
