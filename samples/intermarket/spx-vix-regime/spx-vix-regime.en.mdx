## What Are the SPX and the VIX?

The **SPX** is the S&P 500 stock index. The **VIX** is an index published by Cboe that estimates the **30-day volatility the options market expects** for the S&P 500, expressed as an annualised percentage. It is calculated from option prices, so it reflects what traders are paying for protection and for risk.

<Callout type="warning" title="Corrected and toned down">
  The original said volatility regimes "determine institutional behaviour" and gave optimal strategies by regime. The
  VIX is a **measure of expected size of moves**, not a trade signal or a forecast of direction. Regime bands such as
  15 and 20 are rules of thumb, not standards.
</Callout>

---

## Reading It

A handy approximation: the VIX divided by about **16** (the square root of 252 trading days) is the **typical daily move** the market is pricing, in percent.

| VIX   | Implied typical daily move (VIX ÷ ~16) |
| ----- | -------------------------------------- |
| 12    | about 0.75%                            |
| 20    | about 1.25%                            |
| 30    | about 1.9%                             |

Stocks and the VIX usually move **opposite** each other: when stocks fall quickly, option protection becomes more expensive and the VIX jumps.

## Using It: Size, Not Direction

| Use                                  | How                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Stop distance**                    | Wider stops in high-volatility regimes, tighter in quiet ones            |
| **Position size**                    | Smaller size when volatility is high, so the money at risk stays the same |
| **Context for currencies**           | A rising VIX often goes with risk-off behaviour (see [Risk-On / Risk-Off](/en/intermarket/risk-on-risk-off)) |
| **Strategy fit**                     | Breakout plans often need volatility; mean-reversion plans often prefer quiet markets — test this on your own data |

### Example (no chart) — sizing by regime

You risk 1% of the account per trade. When the VIX is at 12, a stop of 15 pips is normal for your setup; when it jumps to 28, normal swings roughly double, so you widen the stop to 30 pips and **halve the position size** to keep the money at risk the same. Plan with the wider stop: entry **1.0880**, stop **1.0850** (30 pips), target **1.0940** (60 pips): **1 : 2.0** planned. If the VIX falls back below 20, return to your normal stop and size.

## Limits

| Limit                                         | Detail                                                                |
| --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| S&P 500 only                                  | It says nothing about a currency pair's own volatility                |
| Implied, not realised                         | Expected volatility can differ from what happens                      |
| Lag and spikes                                | Spikes can fade within days; regimes shift quickly                    |
| Different markets, different gauges           | Currencies and bonds have their own implied-volatility measures       |
| Thresholds are conventions                    | 15 and 20 are common labels, not rules                                |

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake                                         | Fix                                                        |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Treating a high VIX as "buy" or "sell"          | It measures size, not direction                            |
| Keeping the same stop and size in every regime  | Scale with volatility                                      |
| Using the VIX for a currency's own volatility   | Use that pair's ATR or implied volatility                  |
| Treating the bands as laws                      | Check the regime on your own data                          |
| Reacting to a one-day VIX spike                 | Look at the trend over several days                        |

## Practice

1. For the last year, bucket days by the previous day's VIX (below 15, 15–20, above 20). Compute the average absolute daily move of your pair in each bucket.
2. Check whether your pair's daily range differs across buckets.
3. Rewrite your stop and size rule so it scales with the regime. Log it.
4. Log it in your [trading journal](/en/tools/journal-template).
